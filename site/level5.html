Why it works

Every AI idea has two costs: the effort to set it up and the damage if it goes wrong. The map keeps both in view. Impact is what you gain if it works. Risk is what happens if it quietly fails.

Start in the top left. A quick win teaches your team how to supervise AI while the downside stays small. Big bets are fine later, with a pilot, a cap, and a person watching.

Where a person belongs in the loop

Think of three seats. In the first, the AI drafts and a person decides. In the second, the AI acts and a person reviews a sample. In the third, the AI acts alone. Most marketing work belongs in the first seat for a long time.

Moving a task to the next seat should be earned with evidence: weeks of good results, a cap on the damage, and a clear way to switch it off.

Why it works

Most of the sorting came down to three questions. Whose information is this? Who could be misled? Could a person read it before it goes out? When any answer is shaky, add a human sign-off or stop.

Your own rules matter more than ours. Write them down, name the approved tools, and say who signs off on what. A short written rule beats a long unwritten one.

A one-page rule sheet worth writing

Five lines cover most teams: which tools are approved and for what kind of data, which claims always need review, how AI help is disclosed, who signs off on public replies, and who to ask when unsure.

Review it every few months. Tools and rules both change, and so will your answers.

    Why it works

    A hopeful number is easy: hours saved times pay rate. An honest number takes off what the AI cost you in review, in rework, and in the tool itself. It can come out small. It can come out negative. Either is useful to know.

    Real metrics tie to an outcome or to the time you are buying. Vanity metrics count activity. Report the real ones and say which numbers are estimates.

    Measure before you start

    The easiest way to prove a gain is to time the task by hand for a week before AI touches it. Then time it again with AI, review included. Two honest numbers beat any guess.

    Keep a short note of drafts that needed heavy rewrites. That note is your quality adjustment.
