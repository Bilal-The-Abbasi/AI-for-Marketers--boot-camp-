Why it works

A voice is made of habits you can see: how long the sentences run, how much it jokes, which words it never uses. “Warm and professional” is a mood. “Short sentences, dry, no exclamation marks” is an instruction.

A voice card is those habits in four or five lines, plus one real sentence you wrote. Paste it above any request and the odds shift toward your voice, the same way you saw in Level 1.

Go deeper

Where to find your own traits

Paste three or four of your best past emails or posts into your AI tool and ask: “List the habits that make these sound like one writer. Sentence length, humor, words used, words avoided. Be specific.” Then edit the list until it sounds right to you.

Keep the card short. Five lines the AI follows beat fifteen it half follows. Add a “never” line for anything you have had to delete from drafts more than twice.

Why it works

Examples control style. With none, the AI writes the average subject line. With one, it copies that line’s shape, even its first word. With three, it can see what stays the same and what changes, so it copies the pattern and not the sentence.

A format line controls shape. It tells the AI how to lay the answer out so you can use it without retyping: a table for a spreadsheet, labeled pairs for an email tool, counts to catch long lines.

Go deeper

Good examples, bad examples

Use examples that really worked, not ones you merely like. Make them differ from each other on purpose: different openings, different lengths. If all three start with “Your,” so will the output.

You can also show a bad example. “Do not write like this:” followed by a line you hate is a fast way to rule out a whole style.

Part 2: Break a big job into a chain

Big job: a three-email welcome series for new subscribers of Lantern Hill Garden Center (a made-up shop). One giant prompt tends to give you three generic emails.

Tap the cards in the order you would run them. One card does not belong in the chain.

Your chain

    Why it works

    A vague follow-up gives the AI nothing new to rank, so it shuffles the same words. A specific one names what to keep, what to change, and by how much. Keeping the good parts is as important as fixing the bad ones.

    Big jobs go wrong in the middle where you cannot see. A chain puts a checkpoint after each step, so you catch a wrong direction while it is cheap to fix, and each step feeds the next.

    Check

    Go deeper

    A follow-up formula you can reuse

    Keep: what was right. Change: what was wrong, with a number or an example. Don’t touch: anything else. “Keep headlines 1 and 4. Shorten 3 to 30 characters. Leave the rest.”

    Start a fresh chat when the thread is long and tangled, or when the direction itself has changed. Carry over your voice card and facts, and leave the rest behind.

    The template splits your prompt into two kinds of lines. Fixed lines (role, voice, constraints, example, format, the no-invented-facts rule) stay the same every time. Placeholders in curly braces are the only parts you change.

        Keeping the kit alive
        
    
          
    Save the three templates in one note, one heading each. When a result disappoints, fix the template, not just the output. A new line in the template helps every future run.
