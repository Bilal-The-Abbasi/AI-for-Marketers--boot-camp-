    Write it down before you build anything

    Run the task by hand three times and write the steps in a note. Save one example of a result you were proud of. If you cannot describe a step in a sentence, it is not ready to hand off.

    Start with the task you repeat most and dislike least to explain. A weekly newsletter, a lead follow-up, and a monthly report are all good first picks.

    Meet the Agent

    An agent is an AI that plans steps and uses tools, while you set the goal and the limits. Watch one work, and decide when to let it keep going.

    ObjectiveFollow an AI agent through a task and decide when to approve, edit, or stop it.

    0 / 50 XP

    Mission complete. 50 of 50 XP.

    Try it

    A scripted example. No real AI is running and nothing is actually sent. Fictional business: Tidewater Tea Co., a small iced tea maker.

    Goal you set: Find 3 independent cafes within 10 miles that could stock Tidewater Tea, and draft a short intro email to each.
    Facts you gave it: small-batch, three flavors, free tasting case. Nothing else.
    Limits you set: it may search the web and draft emails. It may not send anything without your OK, and it may not offer discounts.
    1. Goal
    2. Plan
    3. Act
    4. Check
    5. Report

    Why it works

    An agent runs a loop: take the goal, make a plan, act with tools (search, read, draft, send), check its own work, and report. A chat answers once. An agent keeps going until it thinks it is done.

    That is why the limits matter. Reading and drafting are easy to undo. Sending, spending, and publishing are not. Approve the quiet steps fast and slow down at the ones that touch the outside world.

    Check

    Go deeper

    How agents fail in boring ways

    Most agent mistakes are not dramatic. It uses a claim you never gave it, repeats a step, or quietly widens the job. A short written goal, a list of facts it may use, and a list of things it may not do prevent most of it.

    Give a new agent the smallest permissions that do the job. Let it read and draft for a few weeks before it can send anything. Keep its log so you can see what it did and why.

    An automation is a trigger plus some steps. The trick is not building it, it is building it so a bad day cannot hurt you. Watch the risk meter as you go.

    Risk meter

        The test shows what your settings would do with five made-up entries.

        Why it works

        Every automation is a trigger (something happens) followed by actions (do this, then that). Filters sit in between and decide whether the chain keeps going. The AI step is just one link.

        Most bad days come from three things: the same entry arriving twice, an entry with missing or broken data, and a message leaving before anyone read it. A filter, a repeat check, and a draft step cover all three.

        Check

        Go deeper

        Switching on a new automation safely

        Test with made-up entries first, including a messy one and a double. Start in draft mode and read every output for a couple of weeks. Only consider auto-send for the kinds of messages that have stayed clean the whole time.

        Keep one visible off switch, a daily limit, and a log you can read. Any tool you use will have its own names for these. The ideas are the same everywhere.

        Trust Test

        Output that looks fine is not the same as output that is right. Score five drafts two ways, then write the approval rules you will actually use.

        ObjectiveScore AI output two ways and set approval rules you will actually use.

        Mission complete. 50 of 50 XP.

        Try it

        Example: Brightside Pet Supply asked an AI for a Saturday text reminder. Five versions came back. All names, facts, and prices are examples.

        Brief: two sentences, with the time and the price. Voice: friendly, a little dry, no exclamation marks.
        Fact sheet: dog-wash day, Saturday, 10 to 2. $12 per wash. No appointment needed. Nothing else is promised.

        Round 1, the quick look. Five seconds each. Does it look fine, or off?

        0 of 5 scored

        Why it works

        A quick look is good at tone and length. It is bad at facts, because a wrong number reads exactly like a right one. A careful score against the brief and a source is slower, and it is the only thing that catches those.

        You cannot carefully check everything forever. So you match the effort to the risk: a person on anything that makes a promise or moves money, a sample check on the middle, and a log for the low-stakes rest.

        When to tighten the rules again
        
        
          
        Raise your checking whenever something changes: a new prompt, a new tool, a new kind of customer, a new offer. A workflow that was clean for a month is only proven for the conditions it ran in.
        
          
        Keep a short list of the mistakes you catch. If the same one shows up twice, fix the prompt or the checklist, not just the draft.
